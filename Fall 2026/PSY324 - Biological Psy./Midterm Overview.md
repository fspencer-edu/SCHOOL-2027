
# History of Biopsychology

## Early Philosophers

- Aristotle (384-322 BCE)
	- Believed the heart was responsible for thought, emotion, and intelligence
	- Thought the brain primarily cooled the blood
	- Considered the heart the centre of mental activity
- Hippocrates (460-370 BCE)
	- Proposed that the brain controls thoughts, emotions, and behaviour
	- Recognized that brain injuries could affect mental abilities
	- Associated epilepsy with brain dysfunction, rather than supernatural causes
- Plato (427-347 BCE)
	- Believed the brain was the centre of reasoning and intelligence
	- Divided the soul into 3 parts
		- Rational, spirited, and appetitive
	- Associated rational through with the head
- Galen (129-216 CE)
	- Studied anatomy through animal dissections
	- Demonstrated that nerves are connected to the brain and spinal cord
	- Believed the brain controlled movement and sensation
	- Proposed that fluid moving though nerves transmitted information (false)
- Rene Descartes (1596-1650)
	- Proposed mind body dualism
	- Believed the mind and physical body where separate
	- Suggested the pineal gland was where mind and body interacted
	- Described some reflexes as automatic mechanical responses

### Monism vs. Dualism

- Monism
	- Mind and brain are part of the same physical system
	- Mental processes arise from physical activity
- Dualism
	- Mind and body are separate entities
	- Mental experiences cannot be fully explained by physical proesses alone
## Early Neuroscientists

- Julien Jean Cesar Legallois (1812)
	- Experimentally damaged portions of animal nervous system
	- Demonstrated that the medulla is essential for breathing
	- Showed that specific brain regions control vital functions
- Franz Joseph Gall (early 1800s)
	- Developed phrenology
	- Proposed that different brain regions control different personality traits and abilities
	- Incorrectly believed skull bumps could reveal personality and intelligence
	- Prompoted the idea of localization of brain function
- Johann Spurzheim (early 1800s)
	- Collaborated with Gall and popularized phrenology
	- Believed distinct mental abilities corresponded to different brain regions
	- Skull-based personality predications were scientifically invalid
- John Harlow (1848-1868)
	- Documented the case of Phineas Gage
		- Survived an iron rod passing through his frontal lobe
		- Reported changes in personality, behaviour, and decision-making
	- Helped establish the importance of the frontal cortex in behaviour regulation
- Paul Broca (1861)
	- Studied a patient known as Tan
		- Could understand language, but had severe difficulty producing speech
		- Damage to left frontal lobe
		- Broca's area, important for speech production
- Gamillo Golgi (1873)
	- Developed the Golgi stain using silver chromate
	- Allowed individual neurons to be visualized
	- Supported reticular theory, incorrectly believing neurons formed a continuous network
- Santiago Ramon y Cajal (late 1890s)
	- Used Golgi's staining technique to study individual neurons
	- Demonstrated that neurons are separate cells
	- Established the neuron doctrine, the foundation of modern neuroscience
- Charles Sherrington (early 1900s)
	- Studied reflexes and neural communication
	- Introduced the concept of the synapse
	- Demonstrated that neural communication involved excitation, inhibition, and summation
- Ottow Loewi (1921)
	- Experimented on two frog hearts
	- Stimulated the vagus nerve of one heart, slowing its heartbeat
	- Transferred fluid from that heart to a second heart, which also slowed
	- Demonstrated chemical neurotransmission through a substance later identified as acetylcholine

# Invention of Brain Imaging and Recording Methods

- EEG (1924)
	- Electrical activity of populations of neurons using scalp electrodes
	- Great for temporal resolution
- Single unit recording (1920s-1950s)
	- Electrical spikes from individual neurons
- CT scan (1971)
	- Brain structure using X-rays
- PET scan (1970s)
	- Metabolism or molecular activity using radioactive tracers
- MRI (late 1970s)
	- Brain anatomy using magnetic fields and radio waves
	- Great for anatomical spatial resolution
- fMRI (early 1990s)
	- Changes in blood oxygenation associated with neural activity
	- Measures an indirect signal of neural activity, not electrical impulses directly

# What Is Biopsychology

- Biopsychology is the scientific study of how the brain, nervous system, hormones, and other biological processes influence behaviour, emotions, thoughts, and cognition

### 3 Key Points

- Perception occurs in the brain
	- Sensory organs detect information, brain interprets it
- Mental activity and brain activity are inseparable
	- Thinking, memory, emotion, and consciousness depend on neural processes
	- Changes in brain function can change mental experiences
- Differences between people can be traced to brain differences
	- Variations in brain development, structure, chemistry, and connectivity can contribute to differences in behaviour
	- Genetics and experiences both influence these differences

### Common-sense vs. Biological Explanaation

- Common sense
	- Explains behaviour using everyday assumptions, feelings, or personal observations
- Biological
	- Investigates the physiological, developmental, evolutionary, or functional mechanisms underlying aggression

### 4 Biological Explanations of Behaviour

1) Physiological (mechanistic)
	1) How physical processes produce behaviour
	2) Focuses on neurons, hormones, and brain systems
2) Ontogenetic (developmental)
	1) Explains how behaviour develops over an individual's lifetime
	2) Includes genetics, learning, and experience
3) Evolutionary
	1) Explains how behaviour evolved across generations
	2) Focuses on natural selection and ancestral history
4) Functional
	1) Explains how a behaviour contributes to survival or reproduction

# Discovering Neurons

- Reticular theory
	- Proposed that the nervous system was a single continuous network of connected tissue
	- Assumed nerve cells were physically joined together
	- Supported by Golgi
	- Later rejected in favour of the neuron doctrine
- Neuron doctrine
	- Proposed that the nervous system consists of separate, individualized cells called neurons
	- Neurons communicate at specialized connections rather than forming one continuous cellular network
	- Established primarily through Cajal's research
- Golgi stain
	- A silver-based staining technique that colours a small percentage of neurons
	- Reveals the entire structure of individual neurons, including dendrites and axons
	- Made is possible to study detailed neuronal activity
- Cajal's discovery
	- Spanish physician and scientist who studied nervous tissue using microscopy
	- Improved and applied Golgi's staining method
	- Observed individual neurons with distinct boundaries
	- Established that neurons are separate cells
	- Proposed that information typically travels from dendrites and cell body toward the axon

# Anatomy and Function of Neurons and Gila

## Animal cell structures

- Cell membrane
	- Lipid bilayer surrounding the cell
	- Regulates substances entering and leaving
	- Contains proteins, receptors, and ion channels
- Nucleus
	- Contains DNA
	- Controls gene expression and many cellular activities
- Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)
	- Rough ER - contains ribosomes and helps produce proteins
	- Smooth ER - produces lipids, stores calcium, and participates in detoxification
- Ribosomes
	- Produce proteins by translating messenger RNA
	- Can be free in the cytoplasm at attached to rough ER
- Mitochrondria
	- Produce most cellular ATP through aerobic respiration
	- Supply energy for neural signalling and maintaining ion gradients

## Neuron Structures

- Dendrites
	- Branch like structures that receive incoming signals from other neurons
- Cell body (soma)
	- Contains the nucleus and major organelles
	- Maintains the neuron and integrates incoming information
- Axons
	- Long projection that carries action potentials away from the cell body toward terminals
- Afferent
	- Carries information toward the CNS, usually sensory input
- Efferent
	- Carries commands away from the CNS, usually toward muscles or glands
- Axon hillock/initial segment
	- Region where incoming electrical signals are integrated
	- Action potentials are typically initiated at the axon initial segment near the hillock
- Presynaptic terminal/axon terminal
	- End of the axon that releases neurotransmitters into the synapse
- Myelin sheath
	- Fat-rich insulating layer around an axon
	- Increases the speed and efficiency of action potential conduction
- Nodes of Ranvier
	- Gaps between myelin sheath
	- Contain many voltage-gated ion channels
	- Allow action potentials to regenerate during saltatory conduction

## Types of neurons

- Sensory neurons
	- Carry information from sensory receptor to the CNS
	- Skin temperature
- Moto neurons
	- Carry commands from the CNS to muscles
	- A motor neuron causing contraction of bicepts
- Interneurons/intrinsic neurons
	- Connect and process signals between neurons, primarily within the CNS
	- Important for reflexes, sensory processing, and complex computations

## Nerves

- Bundles of axons in the peripheral nervous system
- Surrounded by connective tissue
- Carry sensory, motor, or mixed information
- In the CNS, bundles of axon are generally called tracts

## Glial Cells

![Types of glia - Queensland Brain Institute - University of Queensland](https://qbi.uq.edu.au/files/7740/Types-of-neuroglia_brain-physiology-cells-QBI.png)

- Astrocytes (CNS)
	- Provide structural and metabolic support
	- Regulate extracellular ions and neurotransmitters
	- Help maintain the blood brain barrier
	- Influence synaptic function
- Microglia (CNS)
	- Resident immune cells of the brain and spinal cord
	- Remove pathogens, debris, and damaged cells
	- Participate in inflammation and synapse pruning
- Oligodendrocytes (CNS)
	- Produce myelin around CNS axons
	- One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of multiple axons
- Schwann cells (PNS)
	- Produce myelin around peripheral axons
	- One myelinating Schwann cell typically forms one myelin segment
	- Support regeneration after some peripheral nerve injuries
- Radial glia
	- Help guide migrating neurons during brain development
	- Can act as neural stem/progenitor cells
	- important for organizing the developing nervous system

# Blood Brain Barrier (BBB)

- A selective barrier separating circulating blood from brain tissue
- Formed mainly by tightly connected endothelial cells lining CNS blood vessels
- Supported by pericytes, astrocytes, and surrounding structures
- Protects the brain from toxins, pathogens, and harmful fluctuations in blood composition
- Helps maintain the stable chemical environment neurons need to function properly
- Tight junctions restrict movement between endothelial cells
- Specific transport proteins allow nutrients such as glucose to enter
- Some small lipid-soluble substances can cross more easily
- Many pathogens, drugs, and large molecules are restricted

## What happens when a virus crosses?

- A virus may infect neural or support cells
- Can trigger neuroinflammation and tissue damage
- Server infections can cause encephalitis, neurological dysfunction, or neuronal death
- The effects depend on the virus and immune response

# Neural Signals

- Movement of ions
	- Neural electrical activity depends on the movement of charged particles across the cell membrane
- Ions
	- Sodium (Na+)
		- More concentrated outside the neuron
	- Potassium (K+)
		- More concentrated inside the neuron
	- Chloride (Cl-)
		- More concentrated outside mature neurons
	- Calcium (Ca2+)
		- Much more concentrated outside the cytoplasm

- Ion channels are membrane proteins that allow particular ions to cross the membrane
- Leak channels
- Voltage gated channels
- Ligand gated channels
- Mechanically gated channels
