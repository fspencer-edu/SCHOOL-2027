
# History of Biopsychology

## Early Philosophers

- Aristotle (384-322 BCE)
	- Believed the heart was responsible for thought, emotion, and intelligence
	- Thought the brain primarily cooled the blood
	- Considered the heart the centre of mental activity
- Hippocrates (460-370 BCE)
	- Proposed that the brain controls thoughts, emotions, and behaviour
	- Recognized that brain injuries could affect mental abilities
	- Associated epilepsy with brain dysfunction, rather than supernatural causes
- Plato (427-347 BCE)
	- Believed the brain was the centre of reasoning and intelligence
	- Divided the soul into 3 parts
		- Rational, spirited, and appetitive
	- Associated rational through with the head
- Galen (129-216 CE)
	- Studied anatomy through animal dissections
	- Demonstrated that nerves are connected to the brain and spinal cord
	- Believed the brain controlled movement and sensation
	- Proposed that fluid moving though nerves transmitted information (false)
- Rene Descartes (1596-1650)
	- Proposed mind body dualism
	- Believed the mind and physical body where separate
	- Suggested the pineal gland was where mind and body interacted
	- Described some reflexes as automatic mechanical responses

### Monism vs. Dualism

- Monism
	- Mind and brain are part of the same physical system
	- Mental processes arise from physical activity
- Dualism
	- Mind and body are separate entities
	- Mental experiences cannot be fully explained by physical proesses alone
## Early Neuroscientists

- Julien Jean Cesar Legallois (1812)
	- Experimentally damaged portions of animal nervous system
	- Demonstrated that the medulla is essential for breathing
	- Showed that specific brain regions control vital functions
- Franz Joseph Gall (early 1800s)
	- Developed phrenology
	- Proposed that different brain regions control different personality traits and abilities
	- Incorrectly believed skull bumps could reveal personality and intelligence
	- Prompoted the idea of localization of brain function
- Johann Spurzheim (early 1800s)
	- Collaborated with Gall and popularized phrenology
	- Believed distinct mental abilities corresponded to different brain regions
	- Skull-based personality predications were scientifically invalid
- John Harlow (1848-1868)
	- Documented the case of Phineas Gage
		- Survived an iron rod passing through his frontal lobe
		- Reported changes in personality, behaviour, and decision-making
	- Helped establish the importance of the frontal cortex in behaviour regulation
- Paul Broca (1861)
	- Studied a patient known as Tan
		- Could understand language, but had severe difficulty producing speech
		- Damage to left frontal lobe
		- Broca's area, important for speech production
- Gamillo Golgi (1873)
	- Developed the Golgi stain using silver chromate
	- Allowed individual neurons to be visualized
	- Supported reticular theory, incorrectly believing neurons formed a continuous network
- Santiago Ramon y Cajal (late 1890s)
	- Used Golgi's staining technique to study individual neurons
	- Demonstrated that neurons are separate cells
	- Established the neuron doctrine, the foundation of modern neuroscience
- Charles Sherrington (early 1900s)
	- Studied reflexes and neural communication
	- Introduced the concept of the synapse
	- Demonstrated that neural communication involved excitation, inhibition, and summation
- Ottow Loewi (1921)
	- Experimented on two frog hearts
	- Stimulated the vagus nerve of one heart, slowing its heartbeat
	- Transferred fluid from that heart to a second heart, which also slowed
	- Demonstrated chemical neurotransmission through a substance later identified as acetylcholine

# Invention of Brain Imaging and Recording Methods

- EEG (1924)
	- Electrical activity of populations of neurons using scalp electrodes
	- Great for temporal resolution
- Single unit recording (1920s-1950s)
	- Electrical spikes from individual neurons
- CT scan (1971)
	- Brain structure using X-rays
- PET scan (1970s)
	- Metabolism or molecular activity using radioactive tracers
- MRI (late 1970s)
	- Brain anatomy using magnetic fields and radio waves
	- Great for anatomical spatial resolution
- fMRI (early 1990s)
	- Changes in blood oxygenation associated with neural activity
	- Measures an indirect signal of neural activity, not electrical impulses directly

# What Is Biopsychology

- Biopsychology is the scientific study of how the brain, nervous system, hormones, and other biological processes influence behaviour, emotions, thoughts, and cognition

### 3 Key Points

- Perception occurs in the brain
	- Sensory organs detect information, brain interprets it
- Mental activity and brain activity are inseparable
	- Thinking, memory, emotion, and consciousness depend on neural processes
	- Changes in brain function can change mental experiences
- Differences between people can be traced to brain differences
	- Variations in brain development, structure, chemistry, and connectivity can contribute to differences in behaviour
	- Genetics and experiences both influence these differences

### Common-sense vs. Biological Explanaation

- Common sense
	- Explains behaviour using everyday assumptions, feelings, or personal observations
- Biological
	- Investigates the physiological, developmental, evolutionary, or functional mechanisms underlying aggression

### 4 Biological Explanations of Behaviour

1) Physiological (mechanistic)
	1) How physical processes produce behaviour
	2) Focuses on neurons, hormones, and brain systems
2) Ontogenetic (developmental)
	1) Explains how behaviour develops over an individual's lifetime
	2) Includes genetics, learning, and experience
3) Evolutionary
	1) Explains how behaviour evolved across generations
	2) Focuses on natural selection and ancestral history
4) Functional
	1) Explains how a behaviour contributes to survival or reproduction

# Discovering Neurons

- Reticular theory
	- Proposed that the nervous system was a single continuous network of connected tissue
	- Assumed nerve cells were physically joined together
	- Supported by Golgi
	- Later rejected in favour of the neuron doctrine
- Neuron doctrine
	- Proposed that the nervous system consists of separate, individualized cells called neurons
	- Neurons communicate at specialized connections rather than forming one continuous cellular network
	- Established primarily through Cajal's research
- Golgi stain
	- A silver-based staining technique that colours a small percentage of neurons
	- Reveals the entire structure of individual neurons, including dendrites and axons
	- Made is possible to study detailed neuronal activity
- Cajal's discovery
	- Spanish physician and scientist who studied nervous tissue using microscopy
	- Improved and applied Golgi's staining method
	- Observed individual neurons with distinct boundaries
	- Established that neurons are separate cells
	- Proposed that information typically travels from dendrites and cell body toward the axon

# Anatomy and Function of Neurons and Gila

## Animal cell structures

- Cell membrane
	- Lipid bilayer surrounding the cell
	- Regulates substances entering and leaving
	- Contains proteins, receptors, and ion channels
- Nucleus
	- Contains DNA
	- Controls gene expression and many cellular activities
- Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)
	- Rough ER - contains ribosomes and helps produce proteins
	- Smooth ER - produces lipids, stores calcium, and participates in detoxification
- Ribosomes
	- Produce proteins by translating messenger RNA
	- Can be free in the cytoplasm at attached to rough ER
- Mitochrondria
	- Produce most cellular ATP through aerobic respiration
	- Supply energy for neural signalling and maintaining ion gradients

## Neuron Structures

- Dendrites
	- Branch like structures that receive incoming signals from other neurons
- Cell body (soma)
	- Contains the nucleus and major organelles
	- Maintains the neuron and integrates incoming information
- Axons
	- Long projection that carries action potentials away from the cell body toward terminals
- Afferent
	- Carries information toward the CNS, usually sensory input
- Efferent
	- Carries commands away from the CNS, usually toward muscles or glands
- Axon hillock/initial segment
	- Region where incoming electrical signals are integrated
	- Action potentials are typically initiated at the axon initial segment near the hillock
- Presynaptic terminal/axon terminal
	- End of the axon that releases neurotransmitters into the synapse
- Myelin sheath
	- Fat-rich insulating layer around an axon
	- Increases the speed and efficiency of action potential conduction
- Nodes of Ranvier
	- Gaps between myelin sheath
	- Contain many voltage-gated ion channels
	- Allow action potentials to regenerate during saltatory conduction

## Types of neurons

- Sensory neurons
	- Carry information from sensory receptor to the CNS
	- Skin temperature
- Moto neurons
	- Carry commands from the CNS to muscles
	- A motor neuron causing contraction of bicepts
- Interneurons/intrinsic neurons
	- Connect and process signals between neurons, primarily within the CNS
	- Important for reflexes, sensory processing, and complex computations

## Nerves

- Bundles of axons in the peripheral nervous system
- Surrounded by connective tissue
- Carry sensory, motor, or mixed information
- In the CNS, bundles of axon are generally called tracts

## Glial Cells

- Astrocytes (CNS)
	- Provide structural and metabolic support
	- Regulate extracellular ions and neurotransmitters
	- Help maintain the blood brain barrier
	- Influence synaptic function
- Microglia (CNS)
	- Resident immune cells of the brain and spinal cord

	- Produce myelin around CNS axons
	- One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of multiple axons

- Radial glia
	- Help guide migrating neurons during brain development

	- important for organizing the developing nervous system

# Blood Brain Barrier (BBB)

- A selective barrier separating circulating blood from brain tissue
- Formed mainly by tightly connected endothelial cells lining CNS blood vessels
- Supported by pericytes, astrocytes, and surrounding structures
- Protects the brain from toxins, pathogens, and harmful fluctuations in blood composition
- Helps maintain the stable chemical environment neurons need to function properly
- Tight junctions restrict movement between endothelial cells
- Specific transport proteins allow nutrients such as glucose to enter
- Some small lipid-soluble substances can cross more easily
- Many pathogens, drugs, and large molecules are restricted

## What happens when a virus crosses?

- A virus may infect neural or support cells
- Can trigger neuroinflammation and tissue damage
- Server infections can cause encephalitis, neurological dysfunction, or neuronal death
- The effects depend on the virus and immune response

# Neural Signals

- Movement of ions
	- Neural electrical activity depends on the movement of charged particles across the cell membrane
- Ions
	- Sodium (Na+)
		- More concentrated outside the neuron
	- Potassium (K+)
		- More concentrated inside the neuron
	- Chloride (Cl-)
		- More concentrated outside mature neurons
	- Calcium (Ca2+)
		- Much more concentrated outside the cytoplasm

- Ion channels are membrane proteins that allow particular ions to cross the membrane
- Leak channels
	- Often open and contribute to resting potential
- Voltage gated channels
	- Open or close in response to membrane voltage
- Ligand gated channels
	- Open when a chemical messenger binds
- Mechanically gated channels
	- Respond to pressure, stretch, or movement

## Resting membrane potential

- The voltage different across a neuron's membrane when it is not generating an action potential
- -70 mV
- Inside is more negative that outside
- Most voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels are closed at rest
- K+ leaks channels contribute to the resting voltage
- One electrode is placed inside the neuron and a reference electrode outside

- Electrical gradient
	- Force generated by differences in electrical charge
	- Positive ions are attracted to negative regions
	- At rest, the negative interior attracts Na+ inward
- Concentration gradient
	- Force caused by differences in ion concentration
	- Ions tend to move form higher to lower concentration
	- Na+ tends to move inward
	- K+ tends to move outward
- Sodium-potassium pump
	- Uses ATP to move 3 Na+ ions out and 2+ ions in
	- Maintains the concentration gradients required for signalling
	- Contributes modestly to the negative resting potential
## Action Potential

- Rapid electrical signal that travels along the axon where membrane depolarization reaches threshold
	- Resting (-70 mV)
	- Threshold (-55 mV)
		- Triggers voltage-gated Na+ channels to open rapidly
	- Depolarization
		- Na+ enters the neuron
		- Interior becomes increasingly positive
	- Peak (+30 mV)
		- Membrane voltage reaches it peak
		- Many Na+ channels become inactivated
		- Voltage-gated K+ channels are open
	- Repolarization
		- K+ leaves the neuron
		- Membrane voltage returns toward negative values
	- Hyper-polarization
		- K+ conductance remains temporarily elevated
		- Membrane potential becomes more negative then resting potential
	- Return to resting state
		- Voltage gated channels reset, and resting ion conductances restore the usual voltage
## Refractory Periods

- Absolute refractory period
	- A second action potential cannot be generated because many Na+ channels are inactivated
- Relative refractory period
	- A stronger than usual stimulus is needed to generate another action potential
	- Often overlaps with after hyperpolarization
## Propagation of an action potential

- Depolarization in one axon region triggers voltage-gated channels in the next region
- Signal regenerates as it travels
- Refactor mechanisms discourage propagation backwards
## Saltatory conduction

- Occurs in myelinated axons
- Action potentials regenerate at nodes of Ranvier
- Electrical current travels rapidly through insulated regions between nodes
- Significantly increases conduction speed and efficiency
## All or None Law

- Once threshold is reached, an action potential occurs with a relatively consistent amplitude
- Below threshold, no action potential occurs
- Stronger stimuli do not normally create larger action potentials
- Action potential size
## Rate Law

- Stimulus strength can be represented by action potential frequency
- Stronger stimulation often causes more frequent firing, up to physiological limits
- The amplitude of individual action potentials remains relatively constant
- Action potential frequency

# Synapses

## Presynaptic vs. Postsynaptic Neurons

- Presynaptic neuron
	- The neuron sending the signal
	- Typically releases neurotransmitters from axons terminals
- Postsynaptic neuron
	- The neuron receiving the signal
	- Contain receptors that respond to neurotransmitters
	- May become more or less lively to generate an action potential

- Synapse is a specialized junction where one neuron communicates with another neuron or target cell

## Sherrington's Experiment

- Charles Sherrington studied reflexes
- Movement of limbs in response to stimulation
- Reflexes were not explained by simple electrical conduction through one continuous nerve network

- Reflex arc
	- Reflex arc is a neural circuot that generates a rapid, often involuntary response to a stimulus
- Sensory receptor detects a stimulus
- Sensory neuron carries information toward the spinal cord
- Interneuron processes the signal when present
- Motor neuron carries a command outwrd
- Muscle produces a response

- Stretch reflex
	- Involved a direct sensory-to-motor connection without an intervening interneuron

- 3 observations
	- Reflexes showed a delay
		- Transmission between neurons requires additional time
		- Helped establish the concept of synaptic delay
	- Repeated or combined weak stimuli cloud produce a reflex
		- Individual weak input might not be sufficient
		- multiple inputs could combine to reach the level needed for a response
		- Summation
	- Stimulation of one muscle could inhibit its antagnoist
		- Exciting one motor pathway could suppress an opposing pathway
		- Demonstrated inhibition and reciprocal control of muscles

## Speed of Transmission

- Electrical signals travel along axons relatively quickly
- Chemical synapses introduce a brief delay because neurotransmitters must be released and activate receptors
- Synaptic transmission is therefore generally slower than direct electrical conduction

## Graded Potentials

- SMall changes in membrane voltage, often occurring in dendrites or cell body
- Unlike action potentials, their size varies with the strength if the input
- They decrease in amplitude as they spread passively
- Multiple graded potentials can combine

## Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential (EPSP)

- A graded potential that increase the probability of an action potential
- Commonly causes depolarization, bringing voltage closer to threshold
- Often involved positive ions entering the postsynaptic neuron
- Glutamate activating AMPA receptors
## Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential (IPSP)

- A graded potential that decreases the probability of an action potential
- Often involved Cl- conductance or K+ leaving the cell
- Can cayse hyperpolarization or stablie the membrane voltage through shutning inhibition
- GABA activating GABA-A receptors commonly inhibits mature neurons
### Temporal Summation

- Multiple postsynaptic potentials arrive rapidly from the same synaptic input
- Effects overlap and add together over time
### Spatial Summation

- Postsynaptic potentials from different synaptic locations combine
- Can involved multiple presynaptic neurons firing around the same time
### Spontaneous Firing Rate

- The rate at which a neuron produces action potentials without an obvious external stimulus
- Some neurons have regular ongoing activity
- Excitatory input may increase firing rate
- Inhibitory input may decrease firing rate
### Summation of EPSPs and IPSPs

- The neuron integrates excitatory and inhibitory inputs
- EPSPs generally increase the likelihood of firing
- IPSP generally decrease the likelihood of firing
- If the combined effect reaches threshold at the axon initial segment, an action potential is generated
- EPSP and IPSP
	- Graded responses
	- Can summate
	- Their magnitude varies
	- Usually occur at dendrites and the cell body

- Action potential
	- Regenerative, all or none electrical signals
	- Usually being at the axon initial segment
	- Travel along the axon
	- Carry output information toward synaptic terminals
# Chemical Synapse

- Loewi Frog Experiment
	- Determine whether nerves communicate with the heart through chemical substances
- Isolated two frog hearts
- Stimulated the vagus nerve connected to the first heart
- The first heart slowed
- Transferred fluid from the first heart to the second
- The second heart also slowed
- A chemical released from the first heart's nerve endings affected the second heart
- Neurons can communicate using chemical messengers, not just direct electrical signals
- Acetycholine
## Where can synapses occur?

- Axodendritic
	- Axon terminal connects to a dendrite
	- Very common
- Axosomatic
	- Axon terminal connects to the cell body
	- Often has a strong influence on neuronal output
- Axoaxonic
	- Axon terminal connects to another axon or terminal
	- Can regulate neurotransmitter release
- Dendrodendritic
	- Connections between dendrites
	- Occur in specialized neural circuits
- Neuromusclular junctions
	- Motor neuron communicates with a skeletal muscle fibre
### Components of a chemical synapse

- Presynaptic membrane
	- Membrane of the neuron sending the signal
	- Contains machinery for neurotransmitter release
- Post synaptic membrane
	- Membrane receiving the chemical signal
	- Contains neurotransmitter receptors
- Synaptic cleft
	- Small space separating the cells, often around 20-40 nm wide
- Synaptic vesicles
	- Membrane bound structures storing neurotransmitters in the presynaptic terminal
- Neurotransmitters
	- Chemical messengers released by neurons that bind to receptors on target cells
- Ligand
	- A molecule that binds to a receptor
	- Neurotransmitters are examples
- Ligand-gated ion channels
	- Receptor channels that open or close when a ligand binds
	- Often produce rapid postsynaptic responses

1) Action potential arrives
	1) Electrical signal reaches the presynaptic axon terminal
2) Calcium channels open
	1) Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca+ channels
	2) Ca2+ enters the terminal
3) Neurotransmitter release
	1) Calcium triggers a synaptic vesicle fusion with the presynaptic membrane
	2) Neurotransmitters are released through exocytosis
4) Receptor binding
	1) Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synaptic cleft
	2) Bind to postsynaptic receptors
5) Postsynaptic response
	1) Receptors alter ion conductance or intracellular signalling
	2) The postsynaptic cell may become excited or inhibited
6) Signal termination
	1) Neurotransmitters are removed by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion
	2) Vesicle components can be recycled

- Action potential -> calcium influx -> vesicle fusion -> receptor binding -> postsynaptic response -> neurotransmitter removal
### Neural integration

- The process of combining excitatory and inhibitory signals
- Occurs throughout the neuron, with the axon initial segment playing a major role in action potential initiation
- Determines whether and how often the neuron fires
