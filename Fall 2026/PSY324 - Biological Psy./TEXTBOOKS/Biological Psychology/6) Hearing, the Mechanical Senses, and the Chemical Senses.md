# Hearing
## Sound and the Ear

- Human hearing is sensitive to sounds that vibrate the eardrum by less than one-tenth the diameter of an atom

### Physics and Psychology of Sound

- Sound waves are periodic compressions of air, water, or other media
- Varying in amplitude and frequency
- Amplitude
	- Intensity of a sound wave
- Frequency
	- Number of compressions per sound
	- Measured in hertz (Hz, cycles per second)
- Pitch
	- Related aspect of perception

- Timbre

- Prosody
	- Conveying emotional information by tone of voice

## Pitch Perception

- Frequency theory
	- The basilar membrane vibrates in synchrony with sound waves, causing auditory nerve axons to produce action potentials at the same frequency
	- Refractory period limits the maximum firing rate of any neuron to about 1000 Hz
- Place theory
	- Basilar membrane resemble the strings of a piano
	- Basilar membrane varies from stiff at its base, where the stirrup meets the cochlea
- High frequency sounds excite hair cells near the base
- Low frequency sounds excite cells near the apex

## Sound Localization

- Times of arrival
	- Localizing sounds with a sudden onset
- Intensity
- Sound shadow
	- Produces accurate sound localization for frequencies above 2000 and 3000 Hz

- Phase difference
	- Depends on the frequency of the sound, size of head, and direction of the sound
- Sounds travel faster in water than air
	- Sound arrives at the two ear almost simultaneously and the phase differences are small
- Humans localize how frequencies by phase difference and high frequencies by loudness differences

## Individual Differences

- 4% of people have amusia (tone deafness)
	- Auditory cortex appears to approximate a typical structure, but has fewer than average connections to the frontal cortex
	- Deficit in memory for pitch or attention to pitch
- Absolute pitch

### Deafness

- Conductive deafness (middle-ear deafness)
	- Disease, infections, or tumorous bone growth can prevent the bone of the middle ear from transmitting sounds waves properly to the cochlea
	- Head their own voices
- Nerve deafness (inner-ear deafness)
	- Results from damage to cochlea, the hair cells, or the auditory nerve
	- Inherited, result from disease, or exposure to load noises
- Tinnitus
	- Frequent or constant ringing in the ears
	- Damage to part of the cochlea

### Hearing, Attention, and Old Age

- Brain areas responsible for language comprehension have become less active
- As cochlear hair cells die the auditory cortex receives less input than usual
- Facilitates the excitatory synapses and decreases the inhibitory synapses

## Pain

- Activating certain neurons in the midbrain decreases pain sensitivity in male mice, not not in females
- Pain reliving effects of opiates and cannabinoids differ between males and females

### Stimuli and Spinal Cord Paths

- Pain begins with the least specialized of all receptors, a bare nerve ending
- Axons carrying pain information are thin with no myelin, and conduct impulses relatively slowly
- Mild pain release the neurotransmitter glutamate, whereas stronger pain releases both glutamate and neuropeptides (substance P and CGRP)
- In the spinal cord, one path conveys pain and temperature sensations, and a second path conveys touch
- The pain/temperature pathway crosses immediately from receptors to a tract on the contralateral side of the spinal cord
- Touch information travels up the ipsilateral side of the spinal cord to the medulla, then corses to the contralateral side

- Input to the hypothalamus, amygdala, and anterior cingulate cortex pertains to the unpleasant emotional aspect
- Damage to the somatosensory cortex

- Damage to the amygdala
	- People localize pain, but do not feel it as unpleasant

### Opioids and Endorphins

- Brain puts the brakes on prolonged pain by opioid mechanisms
	- Systems that respond to opiate drugs and similar chemicals
	- Bind to receptors in the spinal cord and the periaqueductal gray area of the midbrain

- Nervous system has its own opiate type chemicals
- Transmitters that attach to the same receptors as morphine are known as endorphins
- Morphine does not affect large diameter axons that convey sharp pain
	- Used for post surgical pain
- Anything that causes pain increases the breathing rate
- Opiates decrease breathing, and produce unwanted effects
	- Sedation, nausea, and constipation

### Other Ways to Relieve Pain

- Gate theory
	- Spinal cord neurons that receive messages from pain receptors also receive input from other inputs that can close the gates for the pain messages
- Cannabinoids
	- Act in the periphery of the body as well as the CNS
	- Reduce inflammation and inhibit the release of glutamate and GABA
- Neuropathic pain
	- A condition of chronic pain that lasts long after the original cause of pain has ended
	- Damaged tissue releases histamine, nerve growth factor, and other chemicals that help repair the damage but also magnify the response of nearby heat and pain receptors
- After injury to sensory neurons, most of the axons from touch receptors fail to regenerate
- Pain axons produce collateral sprouts that take over receptors previously sensitive to touch, such as Meissner's corpuscles
- Capsaicin
	- A chemical in jalapeños
	- Causes a buildup of calcium in the heat and pain neurons
	- Damages neurons' mitochondria, rendering the cells nonfunctional until they regenerate over the next months
- Nerve has 9 types of voltage-gated sodium channels
- Type number 7 (Nav1.7)
	- Important for axons conveying pain and olfactory sensations
	- Needs to cross the blood-brain barrier

### Placebos

- A drug or other procedure with no pharmacological effects
- Relieve pain, depression, and anxiety
- Nocebo effect
	- Opposite of placebo
	- A person experience real physical side effects, pain, or worsened symptoms simply because they expect, fear, or are told a treatment or situation will harm them
- Placebo increases activity in parts of the prefrontal cortex

## Taste

## Synesthesia


- Adaptation
	- Decreased response to a stimulus as a result of recent exposure to it
- Cross-adaptation
	- Reduced response to one taste after exposure to another
- Olfaction
	- The sense of smell, which is the response to chemicals that contact the membranes inside the nose
- Olfactory cells
	- Neurons responsible for smell, located on the olfactory epithelium in the rear of the nasal air passages
- Papillae
	- Structures on a tongue's surface that contain taste buds
- Supertasters
	- People with a higher than average number of taste buds
- Synesthesia
	- The experience some people have in which stimulation of one sense evokes a perception of that sense and another one also
- Taste buds
	- Receptors on the tongue that detect certain chemicals
