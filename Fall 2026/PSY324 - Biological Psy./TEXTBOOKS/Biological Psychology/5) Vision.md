## The Eye and Its Connections to the Brain

- Light enters the eye through an opening in the center of the iris called the pupil
- Focused by the lens and cornea Projected onto the retina
- Light from the left side of the world strikes the right half of the retina, and vice versa
- Light from above strikes the bottom half, and life from below strikes the top half
	- Image inversion

### Route within the Retina

- Messages go from the receptors at the back of the eye to bipolar cells, located closer to the center of the eye
- Bipolar cells send their messages to ganglion cells, located still closer to the center of the eye
- Ganglion cells' axons unite and travel back to the brain as the optic nerve
- Amacrine cells get information form bipolar cells and send it to bipolar cells, ganglion cells, and other amacrine cells
	- Refine the response of bipolar and ganglion cells

<img src="/images/Pasted image 20261026121443.png" alt="image" width="500">

- Receptors send their messages to bipolar and horizontal cells, which send messages to amacrine and ganglion cells
- Optic nerve exits the eye at the blind spot and continues to the brain

- Light passes through ganglion, amacrine, and bipolar cells (transparent) to the receptors
- Blind splot
	- Ganglion cells axons join to form the optic nerve that exists through the back of the eye
	- No receptors
	- Brain fills in the gap
	- Blind spot of one eye is visible to the other eye

### Fovea and Periphery of the Retina

- When fixating on a certain portion of the retina the fovea is used
	- A tiny area specialized for acute, detailed vision
	- Blood vessels and ganglion cells axons are almost absent near the fovea (unimpeded vision)
- Ganglion cells in the fovea of humans and other primates are called midget ganglion cells
	- Small and responds to a single cone
	- Provide 70% of the input to the brain
	- Vision is dominated near the fovea
- Birds' eye occupy most of the head
- Many bird species have two fovea-like areas per eye
- Predatory birds have a greater density of visual receptors on the top half of retinas (looking down) than the bottom half (looking up)

<img src="/images/Pasted image 20261026142007.png" alt="image" width="500">

- In the fovea, each bipolar cell receives excitation from just one cone, and relays its information to a single midget ganglion cell
- In the periphery, input from rods converges onto each bipolar cell, resulting in higher sensitivity to faint light and low sensitivity to spatial location


## Visual Receptors: Rods and Cones

- Retina has two types or receptors
	- Rods
		- Abundant in the periphery
		- Respond to faint light
		- Less useful in daylight because bright light bleaches them
	- Cones
		- Abundant in and near the fovea
		- Useful in bright light and essential for colour vision

- Ratio is higher in species that are active at night
- Both rods and cones contain photopigments
	- Chemicals that release energy when struck by light
	- 11-cis-retinal bound to proteins called opsins

	- Light converts 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal

# Visual Processing in the Brain

- Rods and cones of the retina make synapses with horizontal cells and bipolar cells
- Horizontal cells make inhibitory contact onto bipolar cells
- Make synapses onto amacrine cells and ganglion cells

- Axons of the ganglion cells form the optic nerve, which leaves the retina and travels until the optic nerve from both eye meet at the optic chiasm
- Half of the axons from each eye cross the opposite side of the brain
- Information from the nasal half of each eye crosses to the contralateral hemisphere
- Temporal half goes to the ipsilateral hemisphere

- Most of the optic nerve goes to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus
- Other axons go from the eye to the superior colliculus and part of the hypothalamus

- The primary visual cortex (V1) sends information to the secondary visual cortex (V2)
	- Processes the information further and transmits it to additional areas
	- More elongated
	- Cells respond to corners, textures, or complex shapes
- V2 and V3
	- Some cells are highly responsive to colour, and other helps with stereoscopic depth perception
- V4
	- Cluster of cells responsive to specific colours

## Shape Perception

### The Inferior Temporal Cortex

- Cells in the inferior temporal cortex learn to recognize familiar objects

- Exchanges information with the prefrontal cortex with connections for identifying difficult or ambiguous patterns
- Prefrontal cortex forms hypotheses
- Temporal cortex confirms these hypothesis
- Visual agnosia
	- Inability to recognize objects despite otherwise satisfactory vision
- Fusiform gyrus
	- Responds mainly to faces

- Human newborns come in the world predisposed to pay more attention to faces than other stationary displays

- Prosopagnosia
	- Impaired ability to recognize faces

## Motion Perception

### The Middle Temporal Cortex

- MT (middle temporal cortex)
	- Also known as area V5
- MST (medial superior temporal cortex)
- Receive input mostly from the magnocellular path, which detects overall patterns and movements
- Both colour insensitive

- MT
	- Detect acceleration or deceleration
	- Respond to motion in all three dimensions
	- Photographs that imply movements
- MST
	- Expansion
	- Contraction
	- Rotation
- MT and MST neurons enable you to distinguish between the result of eye movements and the result of object movements

### Motion Blindness

- Motion blindness
	- Being able to see objects, but unable to see whether they are moving, direction, and speed
- Saccades
	- Quick, simultaneous, ballistic jumps of both eyes between two or more focal points
- During a voluntary eye movement, you become motion blind for a split second
