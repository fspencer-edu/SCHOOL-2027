## Terminology to Describe the Nervous System

- CNS
	- Brain
	- Spinal cord
- PNS
	- Connects the brain and spinal cord to the rest of the body
	- Autonomic nervous system (controls body's organs)
		- Enteric nervous system (controls digestive system)

Dorsal = towards back
Ventral = towards the stomach
Anterior = toward the front end
Posterior = toward the rear end
Superior = above another part
Inferior = below another part
Lateral = toward the side, away from the midline
Medial = toward the midline, away from the side
Proximal = located close to point of origin
Distal = located more distant from the point of origin
Ipsilateral = same side of the body
Contralateral = opposite side of body
Coronal plane = a plane that shows the brain as seen from the front
Sagittal plane = a plane that shows the brain from the side
Horizontal plane/transverse plane = a plane that show the brain from above

## The Spinal Cord

- Communicates with all the sense organs and muscles except those of the head
- Segmented structure
	- Left and right sides have a sensory and motor nerve
- Entering dorsal roots carry sensory information
- Existing ventral roots carry motor information
- A neuron cluster outside the CNS is called a ganglion
- A cluster inside the CNS is called a nucleus
- Cell bodies of sensory neurons are outside the spinal cord
- Cell bodies of motor neurons are inside the spinal cord

## The Automatic Nervous System

- The autonomic nervous system consists of neurons that receive information from and send commands to the heart, intestines, and other organs

1) Sympathetic
	1) Vigorous activity
	2) Chains of ganglia left and right of the spinal cord
	3) Fight or flight
2) Parasympathetic
	1) Rest and digest system
	2) Cranial nerves and nerves from the sacral spinal cord
	3) Not arranged in a chain near the spinal cord
	4) Preganglionic axons extend from the spinal cord to parasympathetic ganglia close to each organ

## Thalamus

- The thalamus is a pair of structures in the center of the forebrain
- Most sensory information goes first to the thalamus
	- Processes it and sends output to the cerebral cortex
	- Except for olfactory information
- Related to active memory

## Hypothalamus and Pituitary Gland

- The hypothalamus is a small area just ventral to the thalamus
- Damage leads to abnormalities in motivated behaviours
	- Feeding, drinking, temperature regulation, sexual behaviour, or fighting
- Conveys messages to the pituitary gland
## Basal Ganglia

- A group of subcortical structures lateral to the thalamus
	- Caudate nucleus
	- Putamen
	- Globus pallidus
- Damage impairs movement
	- Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease
- Integrate motivational and emotional behaviour to increase the vigour of selected actions
- Gradual learning of skills and habits
## Basal Forebrain

- Nucleus basalis is on the ventral surface of the forebrain
- Receives input from the hypothalamus and basal ganglia and sends axons that release acetylcholine to widespread areas in the cerebral cortex
- Key system for arousal, wakefulness, and attention
- Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease have impairments of attention and inactivity or deterioration in the nucleus basalis

# The Ventricles

- Nervous system begins development as a tube surrounding a fluid canal
- Central canal becomes the center of the spinal cord, and as the ventricles, 4 fluid filled cavities within the brain
- Each hemisphere contains one of the two large lateral ventricles
- In the posterior, they connect to the third venatical, positioned at the midline, separating the left and right thalamus

<img src="/images/Pasted image 20261021083520.png" alt="image" width="500">

- Cells called the choroid plexus along the walls of the 4 ventricles produce cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
	- Clear fluid similar to blood plasma
- CSF flows from the lateral ventricles to the 3rd and 4th ventricles, and then into the central canal of the spinal cord, and narrow spaces between the brain and the meninges
	- Membranes that surround the brain and spinal cord
- Meninges have pain receptors
	- Meningitis (inflammation)
	- Swollen blood vessels produce migraines
- CSF cushions the brain against mechanical shock when the head moves
	- Provides buoyancy
	- Reservoir of hormones and nutrition for the brain and spinal cord

# The Cerebral Cortex

- The largest part of the mammalian brain is the cerebral cortex
- Cells on the outer surface of the cerebral cortex are gray matter, and their axons extending inward are white matter
- Neurons in each hemisphere communicate to the other hemisphere through the corpus callosum and the smaller anterior commissure
- Primates
	- Monkeys, apes, and humans
- Larger animals have larger brain size, but also larger neurons and fewer neurons per unit of volume

## The Parietal Lobe

- The parietal lobe lies between the occipital lobe and the central sulcus
- Area posterior to the central sulcus, is the postcentral gyrus
	- Receives sensations from touch receptors, muscle-stretch receptors, and joint receptors

<img src="/images/Pasted image 20261021090552.png" alt="image" width="500">

- The postcentral gyrus includes 4 bands of cells parallel to the central sulcus
- Separate areas along each band receive simultaneous information from different parts of the body
- Parietal lobe monitors the information about eye, head, and body positions

## The Temporal Lobe

- The temporal lobe is the lateral portion of each hemisphere, near the temples
- Auditory information
- Left temporal lobe is essential for speech
- Contributes to vision, including perception of movement and recognition of faces and other objects
- Emotional and motivational behaviours
- Damage can lead to Kluver-Bucy syndrome
	- Monkeys failed to display normal fears and anxieties
	- Maladaptive sexual and eating behaviours
- Temporoparietal junction
	- Area where the parietal and temporal lobe meet
	- Receive input from vision, hearing, and body senses
	- Attention, body awareness, and social cognition
	- Damage impairs the ability to image how something would appear from someone else's perspective
## The Frontal Lobe

- Frontal lobe contains the primary motor cortex and the prefrontal cortex
- Precentral gyrus
	- Specialized for the control of fine movements
- Each area along that gyrus controls one part of the body, mostly on the contralateral side
- Areas in the prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal function are known as the default network
	- Dominate activity when not concentrated
	- Daydreaming
	- Think about past, or future
	- Make sense of what people have been saying or doing
	- Storing memories

### The Rise and Fall of Prefrontal Lobotomies

- Consisted of damaging the prefrontal cortex or cutting its connections to the rest of the cortex
- Damaging the prefrontal cortex of primates made them tamer without impairing their sensations or coordination
- Thought to help patients with severe schizophrenia
- Consequences
	- Lack of initiative
	- Memory disorders
	- Distractibility
	- Loss of emotional expression

### Functions of the Prefrontal Cortex

- 3 major regions
	- Posterior portion (movement)
	- Middle zone (cognitive, emotional, and memory)
	- Anterior zone (decision making)

### Beyond Gray Matter - The Connections

- Most researchers have concentrated more on the cell bodies (gray matter), than on the connections among them (white matter)
- Human Connectome Project
	- Effect to map all the long distance connections
	- Correlations between brain connections and behavioural tendencies
	- Abnormal connections responsible for psychological disorders

## How Do the Parts Work Together?

- Binding problem (large scale integration problem)
	- How brain areas produce a perception of a single object
- Early researchers
	- Assumed that various kinds of sensory information converge in areas that associated on sensation with another
- All information does not feed into a central place
- Binding occurs if two you perceive two sensations happening at the same time and place
- Binding might fail if the displays are flashed very briefly or while distracted

# Research Methods

- Methods of relating the brain's structure to its function
	- The effects of brain damage
	- The effects of stimulating a brain area
	- Brain activity during behaviour
	- Correlations of brain anatomy with behaviour

## Effects of Brain Damage

- Paul Broca (1861)
	- Found a patient who lost the ability to speak and had damage in the left frontal cortex
	- Broca's area
- Brain damage affects
	- Recognize faces
	- Perceive motion
	- Pay attention to the left side of the world
	- Motivation
	- Emotion
	- Memory
	- Social behaviour

- Ablation
	- Removal of a brain area
- Lesion
	- Damage by means of a stereotaxic instrument
		- A device for the precise placement of electrodes in the brain
- Injecting a chemical that kills neurons or inactivates them temporarily, without harming the passing axons
	- Researchers can also inject a chemical that disables a particular type of synapse
- Gene-knockout approach
	- Directs a mutation to a gene that regulates one type of cell, transmitter, or receptor

- Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)
	- Application of magnetic stimulation to a portion of the scalp
	- Produces a virtual lesion
	- Silenced the hand area of the motor cortex

## Effects of Brain Stimulation

- Simulation of the brain
	- Insert an electrodes into an animal's brain and deliver brief, mild currents to an area
- Optogenetics
	- Light to control a limited population of neurons
- Invent a protein that responds to light by producing an electrical current
- Certain microbes produce proteins
- A protein react to light by opening a Na+ channel, exciting the neuron
- Another protein react to light by opening a Ca- channel, producing inhibition
- Develop viruses that insert one of these protein into a certain type of neuron
- Develop a thin optical fiber that can shine the right amount of light onto neurons in a narrowly targeted area
## Recording Brain Activity

- Activity increases in some brain areas and decreases in others
- Zebrafish larvae are small and almost transparent
	- Modify fish genetically so that protein in the brain fluoresce when a neuron is active
- Insert electrodes to record activity while a human brain is exposed preliminary to brain surgery
- Electroencephalograph (EEG)
	- Records electrical activity of the brain through electrodes
	- Evoked potentials or evoked responses
- Magnetoencephalograph (MEG)
	- Measures the faint magnetic responses generated by brain activity
	- Temporal resultion
- Positron-emission tomography (PET)
	- High resolution image of activity in a living brain
	- Injection of glucose labeled with radioactive atoms
	- Determines area that have the most glucose and activity
- MRI
	- Record the energy released by water molecules after removal of a magnetic field
- Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)
	- Modified version of MRI based on hemoglobin instead of water
	- Hemoglobin bound to oxygen reacts to a magnetic field differently from hemoglobin without oxygen

- Advantages and disadvantages
	- EEG and MEG
		- Provide precise information about timing
		- Only approximate information about location
		- Sensitive only to cells near the surface of the brain
- PET and fMRI
	- More detailed information about the location fo activity
	- Approximate timing
	- Require more time and effort
	
## Correlating Brain Anatomy with Behaviour

**Key Terms**

- Ablation
	- Removal of a brain area, generally with a surgical knife
- Electroencephalograph (EEG)
	- A device that records electrical activity of the brain through electrodes attached to the scalp
- Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)
	- A modified version of MRI that measures energies based on hemoglobin instead of water; determines the brain areas receiving the greatest supply of blood and using the most oxygen
- Lesion
	- Damage to a structure
- Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
	- Methods of imaging of living brain by using a magnetic field and a ratio frequency field to make atoms with add atomic weights all rotate in the same direction and then removing those fields and measuring the energy that the atoms release
- Magnetoencephalograh (MEC)
	- A device that measures the faint magnetic fields generated by brain activity
- Optogenetics
	- Method of implanting a receptor into a neuron and simulating it with light, to investigate the function of a particular type of neuron
- Phrenology
	- A process of relating skull anatomy to behaviour
- Positron-emission tomography (PET)
	- Method of mapping activity in a living brain by recording the emission of radioactivity from injected chemicals
- Stereotaxis instrument
	- A device for the precise placement of electrodes in the brain
- Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)
	- The application of an intense magnetic field to a portion of the scalp, temporarily inactivating neurons below the magnet
